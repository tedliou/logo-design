# Evidence: what research supports, and what it does not

Read this when a claim about how people perceive a mark goes into a rationale, a reviewer report or an answer to the user. Most findings are lab or survey results whose effect depends on the brand and the context. Use them to choose between candidates, never to overrule the user's taste.

Status: abstract read means the publisher's abstract was opened; secondary means only an encyclopedia, report or database summary; to verify means the claim is from memory or a secondary plan and must be checked against the source before it is quoted.

## Findings in use

| Source | Finding | Status | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Henderson and Cote 1998, Journal of Marketing 62(2) | 195 logos, 13 design characteristics. Marks meant to be recognized correctly do best when very natural, very harmonious and moderately elaborate | Abstract read | Abstraction stops before the subject is lost; the contact sheet's first-glance test guards it |
| Rhodes, Brennan and Carey 1987, Cognitive Psychology 19(4) | Caricatures that exaggerate a face's deviations from the average are identified faster than accurate drawings, as accurately | Abstract read | Feature table: keep and exaggerate the largest deviations (extended beyond faces: inference) |
| Phillips and McQuarrie 2004, Marketing Theory 4(1-2) | Nine kinds of visual rhetoric, ordered by complexity and ambiguity: visual structure (juxtaposition, fusion, replacement) by meaning operation (connection, comparison) | Abstract read for the nine kinds; the names to verify | Form operations fusion and substitution; at most one substitution on the main reading path (inference) |
| Biederman 1987, Psychological Review 94(2) | Objects are recognized from a few geometric components and their relations; concave junctions matter | Secondary | Five primitives or fewer; keep concave corners where parts meet |
| Luffarelli, Stamatogiannakis and Yang 2019, JMR 56(1) | Asymmetric logos help only brands whose personality is exciting; others do better with symmetric ones | Secondary | Symmetry choice cites the brief's feeling words |
| Luffarelli, Mudra and Mahmood 2019, JMR 56(5) | Descriptive logos (showing what the brand does) are easier to process, seem more authentic, and are rated higher; small effect for familiar brands, negative for products with negative associations | Secondary | New brands lean descriptive |
| Jiang, Gorn, Galli and Chattopadhyay 2016, JCR 42(5) | Circular logos suggest softness and comfort, angular ones hardness and durability | Secondary | Corner choice cites the attribute the brand wants |
| Cian, Krishna and Elder 2014, JMR 51 | Static logos that imply motion feel more dynamic and engaging; conservative brands may not benefit | Secondary | Motion operation first when the brief says lively |
| Hekkert, Snelders and van Wieringen 2003 | Designs both typical and novel are preferred most; the two pull against each other | To verify | Reviewer judges typical and novel separately |
| Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman 2004 | Easier-to-process stimuli are judged more beautiful | To verify | Fewer, more consistent construction rules |
| Hagtvedt 2011 | Incomplete typeface logos seem innovative but less trustworthy | To verify | Cuts in letterforms state the trust cost |
| Donderi 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(1) | Compressed file size correlates with judged visual complexity | Secondary | `metrics.mjs` reports gzip size to compare candidates; no threshold |

Nine-grid ideation: Matsumura Yasuo's Mandala Chart (1979, a registered trademark of Clover Keiei Kenkyusho) and Imaizumi Hiroaki's Mandal-Art (1987). One comparison of 19 ideation methods with 100 architecture students (Hirao and Yamamoto 2010, secondary) reports benefits; the evidence is weak, so this skill never says the grid is proven to work.

## Claims not used

Do not write these in rationales, and correct them when the user brings them up:

- Famous marks "built on the golden ratio": the circles are usually drawn on afterwards. Use only the grid you declared.
- "The simpler the better": moderate elaboration did best for recognition and image (Henderson and Cote). The checkable version is "recognizable at small size".
- "Round is friendly, angular is aggressive" as a law: the effect depends on what the brand wants.
- "Symmetric is always better" or "asymmetric is more modern": true only for certain personalities (Luffarelli and others).
- Color psychology lists ("red means passion"): weak evidence, strong context effects.
- "Most people see the FedEx arrow": many never notice it. A hidden negative-space meaning is a bonus, never the basis of recognition.
- "Compression ratio is a beauty score": it measures complexity, not quality.
